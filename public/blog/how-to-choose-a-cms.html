How to choose a CMS for your business: a practical checklist

The right CMS makes your website easy to keep fresh. The wrong one turns every small change into a support ticket. Use this checklist to choose with confidence.

By the WebersLink team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Start with people, not platforms

Most CMS decisions go wrong because they start with a platform someone likes, instead of the people who'll use it every week. Before comparing WordPress, Webflow, Shopify or a headless CMS, answer the questions below. The right platform usually becomes obvious.

1. Who will edit the website?

List everyone who will change content and how comfortable they are with technology. A marketing team that wants to build landing pages needs a visual editor with flexible blocks. A single owner who updates prices twice a year needs something simple and hard to break. Developers editing in code have very different needs from non-technical staff.

  • How many editors, and do some need limited permissions?
  • Do drafts need approval before they go live?
  • Will people edit on a phone?

2. What will you publish?

Write down every type of content: service pages, blog posts, case studies, team profiles, products, events, job listings, locations. Structured content (where each item has the same fields, like a team member's name, role and photo) is easier to manage in a CMS that supports custom content types. If you sell online, decide early whether the store lives in the same system.

3. Which features do you need now, and in two years?

Make two lists. "Now" might be forms, a blog and SEO settings. "Later" might be bookings, memberships, multiple languages or an online store. Choose a CMS that handles "now" simply and won't block "later". Check whether each feature is built in, needs a plugin or extension, or would need custom development.

4. SEO and performance

Your CMS should let you control page titles, meta descriptions, URLs, headings, image alt text, redirects and structured data, and produce a sitemap. It should also make it easy to build fast pages: compressed images, minimal scripts and good hosting. Slow, clumsy sites lose both visitors and rankings.

5. Security and maintenance

  • Who installs updates, and how often?
  • Are backups automatic, and have you tested restoring one?
  • Does it support two-factor login for editors?
  • Is there a log of who changed what?

Hosted platforms handle much of this for you. Self-hosted systems like WordPress give you more freedom but need someone responsible for updates and backups.

6. Total cost of ownership

Look beyond the build price. Add hosting, platform subscriptions, premium plugins or apps, maintenance time and the cost of future changes. A cheap build on a platform that needs a developer for every change can cost more over three years than a well-built site your team can update themselves.

7. Avoid lock-in

Ask how you would leave. Can you export all your content? Do you own the domain, hosting account and code? Are you relying on a proprietary page builder that only one agency understands? Owning your accounts and choosing widely used tools keeps your options open.

Quick guide to common choices

PlatformGood fit when…
WordPressYou want flexibility, lots of ready-made features and a big pool of people who can help.
ShopifySelling products is the main job of the website.
WebflowDesign-led marketing sites edited by a small, visual team.
Headless CMSYou need top performance, a custom front end or content shared with an app.

Your CMS checklist

  1. Editors and permissions listed
  2. Content types written down
  3. "Now" and "later" features listed
  4. SEO controls confirmed
  5. Updates, backups and two-factor login covered
  6. Three-year cost estimated
  7. Exit plan: content export and account ownership

FAQ

Common questions.

What is the best CMS for a small business?

For most small businesses, WordPress is a strong default: flexible, affordable and widely supported. Shopify is better if selling products is the main goal.

How long does it take to move to a new CMS?

A small site can move in a couple of weeks. Larger sites with lots of content, custom features and SEO redirects take longer, so plan the migration carefully.

Do I need a developer to use a CMS?

Not for everyday edits, if the site is built well. You'll want a developer for new features, design changes and maintenance.
