Headless CMS vs WordPress: which should your business use?

WordPress runs a huge share of the web. Headless CMS platforms promise speed and flexibility. Here's what the difference actually is, in plain English, and how to choose.

By the WebersLink team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

First, what is a CMS?

A content management system (CMS) is the editing screen behind a website, where you change text, images, pages and blog posts without touching code. Every CMS stores your content and lets you edit it. The difference between a traditional CMS like WordPress and a headless CMS is what happens next: how that content becomes the website visitors see.

How WordPress works

WordPress is a "traditional" or "coupled" CMS. The editing screen, the content and the website design all live in one system. When someone visits a page, WordPress builds it from your content and your theme, then sends it to the browser. Plugins add features, from contact forms to online stores.

Why people love it: it's familiar, affordable to host, has a plugin for almost everything, and lots of people know how to use it. Where it struggles: plugin-heavy sites can get slow and need regular updates to stay secure.

How a headless CMS works

A headless CMS, such as Sanity, Strapi or Contentful, only stores and serves content. It has no built-in website design: the "head" is missing. Developers build the website (or app) separately and pull the content in through an API. The same content can feed a website, a mobile app and other channels at once.

Why people choose it: very fast websites, complete design freedom, content reused across channels, and a smaller security surface. The trade-offs: it needs developers to build and change the front end, and there's no plugin library to add features in a click.

When WordPress is the right choice

  • You need a professional business website, blog or store without a large budget.
  • Your team wants to build new pages themselves with a visual editor.
  • You rely on ready-made features: forms, SEO tools, bookings, memberships, WooCommerce.
  • You want lots of choice in who maintains the site in future.

When a headless CMS is the right choice

  • Speed and performance are top priorities, for example a high-traffic marketing site.
  • The same content needs to appear on a website, an app and other places.
  • Your design is highly custom and hard to fit into a theme.
  • You have, or can hire, developers to look after the front end.

Common myths

"WordPress is insecure." Most problems come from out-of-date plugins and weak passwords, not WordPress itself. Keeping things updated and adding sensible hardening goes a long way. "Headless is always faster." A badly built headless site can be slow too; a well-built WordPress site can be very fast. "Headless is only for big companies." It's increasingly used by smaller teams, but the build cost is still higher.

Our recommendation

For most small and growing businesses, a well-built WordPress site is the practical choice: affordable, flexible and easy for your team to update. FAQ

Common questions.

Can I move from WordPress to a headless CMS later?

Yes. Content can be exported and moved, and redirects keep your Google rankings. It's a project, so plan it rather than doing it in a rush.

Can WordPress be used headless?

Yes. WordPress can act as the content store while a separate front end displays the site. This keeps the familiar editor with a custom, fast front end.

Which is cheaper to run?

WordPress is usually cheaper to build and host. A headless setup has a CMS plan plus front-end hosting, and changes to the site need a developer.
